# Introduction

A stablecoin targets a reference value, often one US dollar. Its market price can depart from that target when backing, redemption, liquidity, or confidence comes under stress. Understanding the peg means understanding what a holder can exchange the token for and why another participant expects that exchange to work.

On-chain transfer and programmable use can be useful, but neither removes the issuer, banking, contract, or cash-out dependencies around a payment. This article follows those mechanisms and their failure modes. For the underlying transaction model, see [Introduction to Web3](/blog/introduction-to-web3).

# Who actually uses stablecoins

Before going into technical details, it helps to understand who uses stablecoins and why. The answer is more varied than most people expect.

## Crypto traders

One use case is moving between volatile assets and a dollar-denominated token. When a trader wants to exit a volatile position(e.g. they think Bitcoin is about to drop), they sell BTC and move into USDT or USDC. This allows them to **park** value on-chain without converting back to fiat, which would require going through an exchange withdrawal, waiting for a bank transfer, and paying conversion fees. When they want to reenter the market, they simply swap the stablecoin back.

This sounds simple, but it is enormously important. Before stablecoins existed, traders had to cash out to fiat and then back in, which was slow and expensive. Stablecoins made it possible to stay in the crypto ecosystem while managing risk.

## People in countries with unstable currencies

This is arguably the most impactful real-world use case that rarely gets covered in Western tech media.

In an economy with high inflation or restricted access to foreign currency, some users seek a dollar-denominated asset. The relevant comparison depends on local prices, exchange access, legal requirements, and the risk of the particular stablecoin; the conditions differ by country and date.

For many of them, the traditional escape route of converting savings to US dollars is blocked by government capital controls, limited access to US bank accounts, or currency exchange restrictions.

A user may acquire stablecoins through a local provider or peer-to-peer market and hold them in a self-custody wallet. That does not remove exchange restrictions, compliance duties, issuer freezes, or the need to convert into money accepted for local expenses. This article does not estimate how many people use that route.

## DeFi users

Decentralized finance (DeFi) refers to financial services like lending, borrowing, earning interest and trading, that run on public blockchains through smart contracts with no bank or broker in the middle.

Stablecoins are the foundational currency of DeFi. Many lending and trading protocols support stablecoins:

- You can deposit USDC into a lending protocol (like Aave or Compound) and earn interest, at a variable protocol rate, with risks different from an insured bank deposit.
- You can borrow stablecoins against crypto collateral, which is useful if you want liquidity without selling your ETH holdings.
- You can provide liquidity to a decentralized exchange (like Uniswap or Curve) using stablecoin pairs and earn trading fees.

A stable denomination makes it easier to quote debt, fees, and operating costs. It does not eliminate contract, market, or counterparty risk, and protocols can also use other settlement assets.

## Businesses and freelancers doing cross-border payments

A cross-border bank payment can involve correspondent banks, exchange-rate spreads, and operating-hour delays. Costs and speed vary by corridor and provider, so compare an actual route instead of assuming one fee or delay for all bank transfers.

As a hypothetical payment, a company could send 10,000 USDC to a contractor's compatible wallet. Evaluate the chain fee and confirmation first, then the contractor's conversion spread, cash-out fees, access requirements, and time to usable local currency. Fast token delivery is only one stage of that payment.

This is an active and growing use case among remote-first companies, crypto-native businesses, and in the gig economy.

## Protocol treasuries and daos

Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs) — the governance structures behind many DeFi protocols, often hold large treasuries. Keeping those treasuries entirely in volatile governance tokens is risky as a market crash can wipe out months of operating budget. Many DAOs hold a portion of their treasury in stablecoins to cover predictable operating costs (development, audits, grants) regardless of market conditions.

# Why stablecoins matter technically

Beyond specific user groups, stablecoins solve a structural problem that would otherwise make most of web3 impractical.

Consider what happens when you hold volatile crypto in a DeFi protocol:

- You borrow stablecoins against ETH collateral. If collateral value falls far enough relative to the debt, the borrowing position can be liquidated. Merely supplying ETH without borrowing does not create this liquidation mechanism. [Aave borrowing parameters](https://aave.com/help/borrowing/borrow-tokens).
- You want to trade between positions without converting to fiat each time, which involves identity verification, withdrawal delays, and fees.
- You want to pay someone a specific amount, but the value of what you send shifts between the time you send it and the time they receive it.

Stablecoins provide a denomination for these operations, while collateral and redemption risks remain. That supports several on-chain uses:

- **Lending and borrowing**: deposit stablecoins to earn yield, or borrow them against volatile collateral without selling your holdings.
- **Liquidity provision**: stablecoin pairs (e.g., USDC/USDT) on automated market makers earn trading fees while taking depeg, liquidity, and contract risks; similar target prices do not guarantee similar market prices.
- **Cross-border payments**: transfer tokens across a supported chain, then account for any conversion, access, and cash-out stages.
- **On-chain payroll**: denominate contractor payments in a dollar-targeting token; reserves and local cash-out can still depend on banks.
- **Derivatives and structured products**: any on-chain financial instrument that needs a stable unit of account (e.g. options, perpetuals, structured vaults) relies on stablecoins as the settlement currency.

When comparing stablecoin activity with traditional payment networks, distinguish token supply, gross transfer volume, adjusted transfer volume, and payments for goods or services. Exchange movements and repeated transfers are not directly comparable with card purchase volume. This article uses mechanisms rather than a cross-network volume ranking.

# The three designs

Calling a token a stablecoin describes its price target, not a guarantee that it maintains that price. The name does not explain how the target is supported. The mechanism matters enormously, because it determines what risks you are taking on and what can cause the peg to break.

There are three fundamentally different approaches, each making a different set of tradeoffs between trust, capital efficiency, decentralization, and resilience.

A quick note on terminology before we continue: when a stablecoin **loses its peg,** it means the token's market price has drifted away from the \$1.00 target — either above (trading at \$1.05, for example) or, more dangerously, below (\$0.90 or lower). Some depegs are brief and recover within hours while others are permanent. Understanding what causes each type of depeg is central to evaluating any stablecoin.

# Fiat-backed stablecoins

## How they work

In a fiat-backed design, an issuer holds reserve assets intended to back circulating tokens and offers issuance/redemption under specific terms. Those assets may include bank deposits, short-term government securities, and repurchase agreements, rather than one physical dollar sitting in a bank account per token. See [Circle's reserve disclosure](https://www.circle.com/transparency) for one issuer-specific example.

**Arbitrage** is the attempt to profit from a price difference. As a simplified example, an eligible participant could mint 1 USDC for \$1 and sell it at \$1.02, or buy at \$0.98 and redeem at \$1. The quoted difference is before fees, execution changes, and settlement risk. Access is subject to the issuer's eligibility and redemption terms; it is not a risk-free trade available to every wallet. [USDC terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms).

## Major issuers

**USDT (Tether)** launched in 2014 and is used across many trading venues. Its reserve mix and attestations are issuer disclosures, not a substitute for examining redemption eligibility and asset/liability risks. Use [Tether's transparency disclosures](https://tether.to/en/transparency/) for a dated account rather than treating past reserve composition as current.

**USDC (Circle)** launched in 2018. It is issued natively on supported chains; CCTP is one mechanism for moving value by burning and minting native USDC across supported networks. Native issuance and cross-chain transfer are distinct concepts. Check [Circle's USDC documentation](https://developers.circle.com/stablecoins/what-is-usdc) for network support and [reserve disclosures](https://www.circle.com/transparency) for backing.

**PYUSD (PayPal)** launched in 2023, issued by Paxos on behalf of PayPal. Built for consumer use cases, integrated with PayPal's existing 400+ million account base. Fully backed by cash and T-bills.

## The march 2023 USDC depeg

The most significant stress event for fiat-backed stablecoins was the Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) collapse in March 2023. Circle held approximately \$3.3 billion of USDC reserves at SVB. When SVB was shut down by regulators on March 10, USDC briefly depegged to \$0.87 as the market priced in the possibility that a portion of reserves were at risk.

The FDIC guaranteed all deposits the following Sunday, March 12, and USDC recovered to \$1.00. The event revealed two things: fiat-backed stablecoins carry bank counterparty risk that most users had underestimated, and the peg can be preserved even through a severe shock if the underlying claim is ultimately honored.

## The censorship tradeoff

The same regulatory access that makes fiat-backed stablecoins trustworthy also makes them controllable. Both USDC and USDT implement a blacklist: the issuer can freeze any address, rendering the tokens in that address unmovable and non-redeemable.

Circle has used this capability at law enforcement request - for example, to freeze addresses associated with the Tornado Cash smart contracts after the OFAC sanctions in August 2022. From a DeFi composability standpoint, any protocol that accepts USDC as collateral inherits this censorship vector. A governance vote or regulator action could freeze collateral mid-position.

This is not a flaw in the implementation. It is a deliberate design requirement for regulatory compliance. Developers should understand it as a feature with tradeoffs, not a bug.

# Crypto-backed stablecoins

## The core idea

Suppose you own \$1,500 of ETH and want \$1,000 of liquidity without selling the ETH. A collateralized borrowing position can supply that liquidity, but price changes and interest can make the position unsafe. This is a hypothetical mechanism example, not a claim about what a particular bank or protocol will offer.

Crypto-backed stablecoins replicate this logic entirely on-chain. You deposit your ETH into a smart contract, and the contract automatically issues stablecoins to you. You get liquidity without selling. When you want your ETH back, you repay the stablecoins plus a small fee, and the contract releases your collateral.

The key requirement is **overcollateralization**: you must deposit more value than you borrow. If you deposit \$1,500 of ETH, you can borrow up to \$1,000 of stablecoins (a 150% collateralization ratio). The extra \$500 acts as a safety buffer. If ETH's price drops and the buffer shrinks too much, the protocol automatically sells enough of your collateral to repay the debt and protect the stablecoin's backing. This forced sale is called **liquidation**.

## MakerDAO and DAI

DAI was created by MakerDAO and later became part of the Sky ecosystem. The Vault walkthrough below explains the historical Maker mechanism; collateral types, ratios, fees, and migration options are version-specific. DAI has experienced price deviations, including during the March 2023 USDC stress, so its design is not evidence that the peg cannot break. [Sky documentation](https://developers.sky.money/).

### Collateralized debt positions (cdps)

In Maker's system, a CDP (now called a Vault) is a smart contract position. To open a Vault:

1. Deposit collateral (ETH, WBTC, staked ETH, or approved RWA).
2. The protocol assigns a collateralization ratio based on the asset's volatility (e.g., 150% for ETH, 175% for WBTC).
3. Borrow DAI up to the maximum ratio.
4. Pay a **stability fee** (an annual interest rate, set by governance) that accrues on the borrowed DAI.
5. To recover collateral, repay the DAI plus accrued stability fees.

### Price stability mechanisms

Two historical Maker policy levers illustrate supply and demand incentives. They operate alongside mechanisms such as the Peg Stability Module and are not a complete account of the current system:

- **Stability fee**: the interest rate on DAI debt. If DAI trades above \$1.00 (too little supply), governance can lower the stability fee to make borrowing cheaper, expanding supply. If DAI trades below \$1.00 (too much supply), governance raises the fee to incentivize debt repayment, contracting supply.
- **DAI Savings Rate (DSR)**: a yield paid to DAI holders who deposit in the DSR contract. If DAI is trading below \$1.00, raising the DSR creates demand for DAI (holders lock it up for yield), reducing circulating supply and pushing the price up.

### Multi-collateral DAI and RWA

MakerDAO has expanded beyond pure crypto collateral. It now accepts USDC (through its PSM — Peg Stability Module), real-world assets (US Treasury bills held off-chain via structured vehicle), and other tokens. This improves peg stability and capital efficiency but introduces centralization risks: as of 2025, a significant portion of DAI's backing is USDC and US Treasuries, meaning DAI is not fully censorship-resistant.

# Algorithmic stablecoins

## The appeal

These reserve-backed designs commit capital to backing the token: fiat-backed tokens target full reserve coverage, while the crypto-collateral example uses excess collateral. Fiat-backed requires 100% off-chain reserves held in a bank. Crypto-backed requires 150% or more in on-chain collateral. Either way, you need capital sitting idle just to create the stablecoin.

Algorithmic stablecoins try to escape this constraint. The idea is to maintain the peg purely through software logic and market incentives, without requiring full collateral backing. Reducing external collateral is the proposed efficiency gain. Whether control is decentralized or reserves can be seized depends on the implementation; an algorithmic label does not decide those properties.

The UST example shows how a confidence-dependent mechanism can fail when redemption expands the supply of its volatile counterpart.

## The seigniorage mechanism

The dominant algorithmic design uses a two-token model:

- A **stablecoin** targeting \$1.00 (e.g., UST).
- A **volatile governance/seigniorage token** that absorbs volatility (e.g., LUNA).

The mechanism:

- If the stablecoin trades above \$1.00, users can burn \$1.00 of the volatile token to mint one stablecoin unit with a \$1 target. The minted stablecoin is then sold for profit, expanding supply and pushing the price back to \$1.00.
- If the stablecoin trades below \$1.00, users can burn one stablecoin unit (target value \$1) to receive \$1 worth of the volatile token at the protocol's quoted rate. The burned stablecoin contracts supply, while the minted volatile token is sold for profit, pushing the stablecoin back to \$1.00.

## The ust/luna collapse

Terra's UST and LUNA were the most prominent algorithmic stablecoin pair. At peak, UST had a market cap of ~\$18 billion with the Anchor Protocol offering 20% APY on UST deposits — a yield that could only be sustained by continuous inflows of new capital, the hallmark of an unsustainable structure.

In May 2022, a large holder began selling UST in size. The price fell below \$1.00. To restore the peg, holders burned UST to mint LUNA. As the LUNA supply rapidly inflated, its price fell. This triggered more panic selling of both UST and LUNA — the reflexive death spiral.

UST lost its dollar peg in May 2022 while LUNA supply expanded and its market price collapsed. ### Why pure algorithmic designs fail

The fundamental problem is **reflexivity**. In a fiat-backed system, the peg is maintained by a claim on real assets. In a crypto-backed system, the peg is maintained by excess collateral value. In a purely algorithmic system, the peg is maintained entirely by confidence that other participants will continue to trust and use the mechanism.

When confidence holds, the mechanism is self-reinforcing. When confidence breaks, the mechanism inverts: the same arbitrage that maintained the peg now accelerates its collapse. There is no external anchor to absorb the sell pressure.

UST demonstrates that confidence in a volatile redemption asset can disappear faster than the peg mechanism absorbs selling. Evaluate a proposed design's backing, redemption limits, liquidity, and stress assumptions rather than relying on the word algorithmic as either a guarantee or a complete explanation.

# Comparing the designs

These are mechanisms, not mutually exclusive product certifications. A token can combine crypto collateral, centralized reserve assets, and governance controls.

| Design                   | Backing or stabilization mechanism          | Dependencies to inspect                                                 |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fiat-backed              | Issuer reserves and eligible redemption     | Reserve quality, banking access, issuer permissions, redemption terms   |
| Crypto-collateralized    | Collateralized debt and liquidation         | Collateral mix, oracle, contract permissions, liquidation liquidity     |
| Algorithmic / endogenous | Supply incentives, sometimes a paired token | External backing, redemption limits, demand and confidence under stress |

Legal status and censorship resistance depend on the actual issuer, jurisdiction, collateral, and control rights. Neither a row label nor a collateral ratio establishes those properties.

# Oracles: the critical dependency

Crypto-backed stablecoins depend on accurate price feeds to determine collateralization ratios and trigger liquidations. These **price oracles** are a critical attack surface.

An attacker who can manipulate the oracle can trick the protocol into accepting under-backed positions or triggering liquidations incorrectly. Oracle manipulation attacks have caused hundreds of millions in losses across DeFi.

Robust oracle designs use:

- **Chainlink**: a decentralized network of independent data providers that aggregates and signs price data. The most widely used.
- **TWAP (time-weighted average price)**: calculated from on-chain DEX trading data over a time window. Harder to manipulate than spot prices since a sustained price distortion is required.
- **Maker's Oracle Security Module (OSM)**: introduces a 1-hour delay between the reported price and the price used by the protocol.

# Regulatory landscape

**United States:** the GENIUS Act became Public Law 119-27 on 18 July 2025. It establishes a framework for permitted payment-stablecoin issuers, including reserve and supervisory requirements. Enactment, effective dates, and implementing regulations are different milestones; check the statute and regulator guidance for the activity and date you are assessing. This replaces the older description of a proposal awaiting Senate action. [Official law text and record](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1582/text/pl).

**European Union:** MiCA has distinct rules for asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens. The stablecoin-related provisions began applying in June 2024, with broader MiCA provisions applying from December 2024. Requirements depend on the category and issuer, so do not reduce all stablecoins to a single money-transmitter classification. [European Commission MiCA overview](https://finance.ec.europa.eu/digital-finance/crypto-assets_en).

This is dated context checked in October 2026, not a determination that a token or planned activity complies with the applicable rules.

# Risks summary

Understanding the risk profile of each stablecoin type is essential before using them in protocols or accepting them as payment:

**Fiat-backed risks:**

- Custodian insolvency (SVB in March 2023).
- Reserve opacity (Tether's historical disclosure issues).
- Address blacklisting (censorship by issuer or regulator).
- Regulatory action against the issuer.

**Crypto-backed risks:**

- Cascading liquidations during sharp market drops.
- Oracle manipulation or failure.
- Smart contract bugs (code is immutable in protocols like Liquity).
- Governance attacks (MakerDAO's governance token can be acquired to pass malicious proposals).

**Algorithmic risks:**

- Death spiral under any meaningful confidence crisis.
- Unsustainable yield programs masking structural inviability.
- No recovery mechanism once confidence is lost.

# Conclusion

Stablecoins share a price target, but their backing and redemption mechanisms create different dependencies and failure modes. Understanding those mechanisms explains both how a peg is supported and how it can break.

Before using a stablecoin, identify the issuer or protocol, what backs it, who can redeem it, and what can interrupt that process. For a payment, include the purchase and cash-out stages. For a borrowing position, include collateral and liquidation risk.

A dollar target simplifies denomination. It does not make the token equivalent to a guaranteed dollar balance or an insured deposit.
